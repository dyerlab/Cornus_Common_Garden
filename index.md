Dear Editor,

On behalf of my co-authors, Jane Remfert, Andrew Eckert, and me, I am pleased to submit our original research manuscript titled “Early fitness consequences of cultivar gene escape in flowering dogwood (*Cornus florida* L., Cornaceae)” for publication as a Research Article in *Conservation Genetics*.

Ornamental cultivars of native plants are increasingly common in urban and residential landscapes, creating substantial genetic reservoirs adjacent to wild populations. In eastern North American forests, native flowering dogwood (*C.* *florida*) populations face severe demographic pressures from the fungal pathogen Discula destructiva and invasive competition. Although cultivars have been bred for enhanced growth vigor and floral display in high-light environments, it remains unknown whether offspring of cultivar origin experience severe physiological maladaptation when recruiting into natural, deeply shaded forest understories.

To test the competing maladaptation and cultivar persistence hypotheses, we monitored 569 open-pollinated seedlings from 36 maternal families (20 urban/cultivar and 16 native forest) over two growing seasons in an unmanipulated, deeply shaded understory common garden at the VCU Rice Rivers Center. Key findings of our study include:

- *High Early Survival Across Origins:* Overall seedling survival was \~82% and did not differ significantly between native-origin and cultivar-origin seedlings, nor by continuous genetic admixture percentage (AP).

- *Heritable Growth Variation in Cultivar Lineages:* Cultivar-origin families exhibited significant narrow-sense heritability for height, above-ground biomass, and stem biomass, whereas native families exhibited no detectable heritable variation across measured growth traits.

- *Lack of Understory Fitness Barrier:* Cultivar-origin seedlings were not competitively disadvantaged in a deeply shaded understory, demonstrating that early viability selection is unlikely to prevent the establishment and long-term introgression of escaped cultivar alleles into wild populations.

These findings provide critical empirical insights into the evolutionary implications of urban-wild gene flow, localized genetic homogenization, and the conservation of wild genetic diversity in declining native tree species. Given *Conservation Genetics’* focus on applying evolutionary and population genetics to conservation biology and species management, we believe our manuscript is a strong fit for your readership.

We confirm that this manuscript presents original research, is not currently under consideration elsewhere, and has not been published previously. All authors have reviewed the manuscript and approved its submission to *Conservation Genetics*. The authors declare no competing interests. All sampling was conducted under appropriate state and institutional permits (Pocahontas State Park and VCU Rice Rivers Center permit PO-RCP-102617), and all underlying data and annotated analysis scripts are publicly accessible.
